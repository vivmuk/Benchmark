World Models in Medical Affairs

Generative systems that learn how disease, evidence, and human behavior actually behave are about to reshape the most human-facing function in pharma.

An essay on the next decade • Strategy & Scientific Communication • ~14 min read

Medical Affairs has always been the function that translates. It stands between the clean abstraction of a clinical trial and the messy reality of practicing clinicians, health systems, payers, and patients. Its currency is evidence, and its craft is judgment about how that evidence lands in the world. For thirty years, the tools have been static: slide decks, publications, advisory boards, a well-briefed field team. World models threaten — and promise — to change the substrate underneath all of it.

To understand why, it helps to be precise about what a world model actually is, because the term is thrown around loosely.

What we mean by a world model

A world model is an AI system that learns a compressed, internal representation of how some domain evolves — the latent dynamics, causal structure, and counterfactual behavior of a system — so that it can simulate, predict, and plan rather than merely retrieve or classify. Unlike a chatbot that maps text to text, a world model maps states to plausible next states. It can answer "what would happen if…" not just "what has been written about…"

That distinction is the whole story. A large language model can summarize the literature on a therapy. A world model of a disease can simulate a cohort of virtual patients progressing through that disease under different interventions, generating the evidence the literature doesn't yet contain. One reflects the world as recorded; the other rehearses the world as it might unfold.

Medical Affairs is, at its core, a discipline of anticipation: anticipating what clinicians will ask, what evidence gaps a launch will expose, how a health system will respond to a new standard of care. Anticipation is exactly what world models are built to do.

01The Applications

Let's move from the abstract to the concrete. Below are the domains where world models will find purchase first — not because they are the most glamorous, but because the value is legible and the data already exists.

Disease and patient-journey simulation

The most direct application is a generative model of disease progression. Trained on registries, electronic health records, claims, and trial data, such a model can produce synthetic patient cohorts whose trajectories are statistically faithful to real populations. Medical teams can then ask questions that no single dataset answers: How does the addition of a new therapy shift the natural history of a subpopulation? Where in the journey does diagnostic delay do the most damage? Which comorbidity clusters change the risk calculus?

This is not idle simulation. It reframes unmet need from a static bullet point on a strategy slide into a dynamic, queryable object.

In-silico evidence generation

Evidence generation is Medical Affairs' heaviest lift and its longest timeline. World models compress it. Synthetic control arms, already accepted in narrow regulatory contexts, are the primitive form. The mature form is a model that can pressure-test a real-world evidence study before a single site is opened — estimating power, surfacing likely confounders, and flagging where the observed data will diverge from the assumed dynamics. Studies get designed against a simulated reality first, and only the survivors reach the field.

The next generation of evidence will be rehearsed in simulation before it is ever collected in the world. The operating premise of world-model-driven evidence

Scientific-narrative stress-testing

Every launch rests on a scientific narrative — a chain of claims connecting mechanism to outcome to value. World models let teams war-game that narrative against simulated skeptics: a model of a cardiologist's likely objections, a model of a payer's evidence thresholds, a model of how a competitor's data will reframe the conversation. This is red-teaming for medical storytelling, and it turns the advisory board from the only feedback mechanism into the final one.

Field medical intelligence and MSL augmentation

The Medical Science Liaison is the human sensor network of the industry. A world model can act as the liaison's co-pilot — not a script, but a simulator. Before a key scientific exchange, an MSL can rehearse against a model of a specific KOL's published positions and probable questions. After the interaction, the same model helps interpret the signal: is this one physician's idiosyncrasy, or the leading edge of a shift in clinical thinking? Aggregated, these interactions feed back into a living model of the scientific community's evolving beliefs.

Personalized medical education

Static CME and one-size-fits-all decks assume a homogeneous audience. A world model of a learner — their specialty, their prior knowledge, their misconceptions — enables education that adapts in real time, closing genuine knowledge gaps rather than broadcasting into them. The scientific content stays rigorously the same; the path through it becomes individual.

Health-system and access modeling

Finally, the widest lens: modeling how an intervention propagates through a health system. Uptake, guideline adoption, referral pattern shifts, budget impact — these are dynamical, feedback-laden processes that spreadsheets caricature and world models can actually represent. Medical Affairs gains the ability to reason about its impact at the level of populations and systems, not just individual engagements.

The through-line

Every one of these applications shares a shape: replacing a static artifact (a study, a deck, a briefing, a curriculum) with a queryable dynamical system. That is the actual transformation. The deliverables are downstream.

02The Transformation

Applications are additive; transformation is structural. If world models merely made existing tasks faster, they'd be a productivity story. They are more than that because they change what Medical Affairs is for. Four shifts define the arc.

From reactive to anticipatory

The deepest change is temporal. Today, Medical Affairs largely responds — to questions, to competitive data, to emerging safety signals. A function equipped with world models operates ahead of the curve, because simulation lets it inhabit likely futures before they arrive. The strategic value of the function shifts from answering well to anticipating correctly.

From artifacts to living models

The publication, the platform slide, the medical information response — these become renderings of an underlying model rather than the primary work product. When the model updates, every downstream artifact can update with it. Consistency stops being a governance headache enforced by review committees and becomes a property of the system.

The human center of gravity moves

None of this removes the human; it relocates them. Judgment about what to simulate, skepticism about what a model asserts, the relationships that make a KOL trust a scientific exchange, and the ethical responsibility for what gets communicated — these become more central, not less. The MSL of the coming decade is less a walking database and more a scientist who interrogates models, validates their outputs against clinical reality, and carries the trust that no simulation can generate.

Automate the retrieval, elevate the judgment. The function that thrives will be the one that treats the model as a colleague to be challenged, not an oracle to be obeyed.

03The Hard Parts

An honest essay names its own failure modes. World models in Medical Affairs face constraints that are not merely technical — they are regulatory, ethical, and epistemic, and they are severe.

  • Fidelity and hallucination. A world model that confidently simulates a plausible-but-wrong disease trajectory is more dangerous than a chatbot that fabricates a citation, because its output looks like evidence. Validation against ground truth cannot be optional.
  • Regulatory acceptance. The line between internal decision-support and externally communicable evidence is bright and heavily policed. Simulated cohorts can inform strategy long before regulators will accept them in a label. Confusing the two is a compliance catastrophe.
  • Data provenance and bias. A world model inherits the blind spots of its training data. Populations underrepresented in registries and trials will be underrepresented in the model's imagined futures — entrenching, at scale, the very inequities Medical Affairs should be surfacing.
  • The separation of church and state. Medical Affairs' credibility rests on its non-promotional independence. Any world model touching medical work must be firewalled — in governance and in fact — from commercial incentives, or the function forfeits the trust that is its entire reason to exist.
  • Interpretability. A recommendation a medical director cannot explain to a regulator, an ethics committee, or a physician is a recommendation they cannot use.
The governing principle

Treat every world model as a hypothesis engine, not a truth engine. Its job is to generate sharper questions and better-designed studies — not to substitute simulated evidence for the real thing. The moment that boundary blurs, the science is compromised.

04What To Do About It

For leaders who find this persuasive rather than speculative, the near-term moves are unglamorous and correct:

  • Invest in data infrastructure before models. A world model is only as good as the longitudinal, well-governed data it learns from. This is the constraint, and it is boring, and it is where the winners will separate from the pretenders.
  • Pilot in low-stakes, high-learning domains first — internal study design, MSL rehearsal, insight synthesis — where errors are caught before they reach a physician or a patient.
  • Build validation into the workflow, not around it. Every simulated output should carry its uncertainty and its provenance as first-class attributes.
  • Reskill the field. The scarce talent of the next decade is the medical professional who is fluent in both clinical science and model interrogation. Start growing them now.
  • Write the governance before the pressure to cut corners arrives. Decide, in the cold light of day, where the line between decision-support and evidence sits.

05The Bet

Here is the plainest version of the argument. Medical Affairs exists because there is a gap between what a trial proves and what the world needs to know. World models are the first tools that can model that gap directly — simulating the futures, the objections, the patients, and the systems that the evidence base has not yet caught up to.

Used carelessly, they will manufacture false confidence at industrial scale. Used well, they will let the most scientifically rigorous function in the industry think faster, anticipate further, and serve patients better — not by replacing scientific judgment, but by giving it a far larger canvas to work on.

The transformation will not announce itself with a single product launch. It will arrive the way most real change does: quietly, as the static artifacts of today are gradually replaced by living models, until one day the question "what would happen if?" is one the function can actually answer.

Medical Affairs has always translated evidence into understanding. World models let it translate uncertainty into foresight — and that is a different, and larger, job.
